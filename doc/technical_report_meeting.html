§1 — THE PROBLEM

Value, speed, and hidden hands, under an 8-fan floor

Chinese Standard Mahjong is 4-player and imperfect-information, with a defining constraint: a hand may only be declared a win if it scores at least eight fan.

Most locally-legal hands are simply not winnable, so every decision trades value (reaching the 8-fan floor) against speed and width (having enough winning tiles to complete before an opponent does) — all under hidden opponent hands and a shrinking wall.

The decisive Stage-2 ran the duplicate format: each wall is played under all 24 seat permutations and scores summed, so seat and deal luck are engineered out and the ranking reflects relative strength. Two format facts shaped the whole race: the per-decision budget is generous (~5000 ms), and the final ranks bots by cumulative score, not mean placement — a bot that reliably parks at 2nd is not rewarded like one that occasionally wins big.

§2 — OUR SOLUTION

Imitation, then ensemble

kdens3 is deliberately not a search or RL agent. It is a behavior-cloning policy distilled from strong play, built to reproduce expert decisions reliably, at zero error rate.

Architecture

ResNet-BN, 128 × 40

A residual CNN with batch-norm, 128 channels × 40 blocks, over ~38 feature planes (hand, melds, discards, public state). A legal-move mask means it only ever proposes valid actions.

Training signal

Distillation of top play

Supervised imitation of decisions harvested from strong Botzone players — matching the teacher's action distribution, not merely its argmax.

Deployment

3-net mean-softmax ensemble

Three independently-seeded nets; average the softmax-over-legal-moves, then act. Smoother tails, no single-net blind spots — at ~1000 ms/move, well inside budget.

The real edge

A measurement stack that doesn't lie

Bias-corrected paired-wall gates, null-calibration to exactly 2.500, 586× batched inference, and automated real-field matches vs the actual finalists.

Why this design earned a podium

Zero-error reliability. 0 illegal moves and 0 timeouts across 12,288 games — while many ladder bots suffer 15–21% error-endings, which are fatal in a cumulative-score format. Champion-level defense, learned implicitly from experts who avoid dangerous discards, with no bolted-on defense module (we tried several; all hurt). And low variance by construction — the format's winner won on consistency, not peak.

§5 — WHAT WE LEARNED

The lessons, including the ones that cost the most

A The imitation ceiling

Imitating expert play caps you at expert level, and in MCR that cap is extraordinarily hard to exceed. Every component we could build — policy, value head, belief, rollout — is itself learned from the same imitation data, so none can exceed the teacher. The oracle reaches 3.55 only by cheating (seeing hidden tiles); that signal survives no deployable approximation. "Deeper rollout hurts" is the tell: the rollout policy is the binding limiter.

B The evaluation wall

The in-house gate understated real defensive exposure by 6–8× because it evaluated against weak proxies on a score-EV metric that cannot price variance-reduction. Treat every in-house number as a hypothesis until confirmed against the real field.

C Validation accuracy ≠ playing strength

Two chess student-ensembles with identical val-top1 (0.445) had playing strengths of 0.208 vs 0.054 — a swing invisible to accuracy. We now gate only on the task metric, with a confidence interval.

D Verification beats cleverness

Our discipline caught ~8 false-positive "improvements" before they became claims: overlapping evaluation blocks manufacturing significance, estimator self-selection faking a positive, and a silent harness no-op that returned exactly 2.500. Blocks must be disjoint; the gate region must be disjoint from the selection region; pairing and disjointness are separate obligations.

E A good imitation net already does the obvious

The human-knowledge features we tried to inject — fan-awareness, meld defense, wait-width — were already present in the policy (it matched expert danger labels 98.65%). Injecting a feature for behavior the expert already exhibits adds nothing in-distribution.

§6 — THE DOORS WE COULD NOT OPEN

Where a 2027 team should push

We exhausted the deployable-imitation space. These are the levers with real upside that we could not walk through within our compute budget — ranked.

  1. From-scratch superhuman self-play RL, at scale

    Our league plateaued, but the plateau is structural (near-optimal SL → tight trust region → weak self-play gradient), not proven fundamental. Highest upside — and the JAX infrastructure is ready.

  2. A better data source

    Everything we have is teacher-capped. Higher-tier human data, or a self-play corpus that provably exceeds the current expert, is the only thing that moves the ceiling itself.

  3. Win-conversion as an explicit objective

    The final names the gap: turn safe 2nds into 1sts via higher-value hand construction. This is a construction problem — build the shape 2–3 shanten earlier — not a discard-overlay or search problem. Both of those fire too late, which is why every overlay was a no-op.

  4. Accuracy-first PIMC within the 5 s budget

    The batched engine is built; every deployable config tied. The 5 s budget permits far more worlds and deeper rollouts than we ran. Honest prior: still a tie — but it is the one search variant left un-maxed.

We built a reliable, champion-grade imitation ensemble and finished second of sixteen in a statistical tie for first — beating the champion on defense, losing only on win-value conversion in a format that rewards it. Behind that one clean model is a disciplined graveyard of ~32 rigorously-killed levers, and the campaign's real deliverable is the pair of hard-won lessons: imitation caps you at the teacher, and in MCR that ceiling survives search, RL, and every static heuristic we could build — and the evaluation you trust is probably lying to you by 6–8× until you measure against the real field. We are proud of the podium; we are prouder of the false positives we caught before they became claims.
